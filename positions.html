Positions

Six positions, and nobody expects you to know them yet.

Every listing that says “need an oppo” is asking a question you can't answer on your first night. Here is what each position actually does, what it means if you're new, and the jargon that gets used without anyone stopping to explain it.

Back of court

First, the numbers aren't positions.

The six spots on the floor are numbered 1 to 6, and everyone shifts one place clockwise each time their team wins the serve back. Zone 1 serves.

So “middle back” is a place — zone 6. “Middle blocker” is a position — a job. A middle blocker standing in zone 6 is in the back row and cannot attack from up at the net. This is the single most common thing beginners get tangled in, and it isn't your fault: the same word is doing two jobs.

The three front zones are 4, 3 and 2. The three back zones are 5, 6 and 1. That's the whole system.

The six positions

Read the “if you're new” line first. It's the one that decides whether a spot is worth trying on a Tuesday.

OH

Outside hitter

Also called: left side, pin hitter, “outside”

The busiest attacking spot, and the one most people start in. You hit from the left antenna and you also pass in the back row, so you touch the ball constantly.

Where
Front left, and all three back-row spots as you rotate.
The job
Takes the most swings of anyone, including the awkward ones when the pass is bad.
If you're new
A good first hitting spot. The set comes high and slow, which gives you time to see it.
What you'll hear
“We need an outside” means they want someone who can swing from the left.
OPP

Opposite

Also called: oppo, right side, RS

Named for where they stand in the rotation — always opposite the setter. You attack from the right side and you're the main blocker against the other team's outside hitter.

Where
Front right, diagonally across from the setter at all times.
The job
Attacks from the right and blocks. Usually kept out of serve receive.
If you're new
Less passing pressure than outside, but you hit from your off-hand side, which takes a while to feel normal.
What you'll hear
“Oppo” and “right side” are the same job. Nobody will explain that.
MB

Middle blocker

Also called: middle, middle hitter, MH

You start at the net in the middle and move sideways to block whichever hitter swings. On offence you hit fast, low sets that beat the block.

Where
Front middle. Often substituted out when you rotate to the back row.
The job
Reads the setter and gets a block up. Hits quick sets timed before the ball is even set.
If you're new
The hardest spot to start in. It's almost entirely timing and lateral movement, both of which take months.
What you'll hear
“Middle” means the position. “Middle back” means a place on the floor. Different things.
S

Setter

Also called: “the setter”, second contact

You touch nearly every ball your team plays. The pass comes to you, and you choose which hitter gets it and how fast.

Where
Front right when in the front row, then back right. Runs to the ball from wherever they are.
The job
Delivers the second contact, calls the offence, and takes the blame for everything.
If you're new
The highest-responsibility spot on the court. In pickup, the most experienced player usually takes it — and that's fine.
What you'll hear
“Run a 5-1” means one setter plays all six rotations. You do not need to know this to play.
L

Libero

Pronounced LEE-beh-ro, or LIB-uh-ro. Both are used.

A defensive specialist with their own rulebook. They wear a contrasting jersey, play only in the back row, and can swap in and out without using a substitution.

Where
Back row only. Never rotates to the front.
The job
Passes serve and digs attacks. Cannot block, cannot attack a ball that's above the height of the net, cannot set a front-row attacker overhand from inside the front zone.
If you're new
Uncommon in pickup. The different jersey confuses every newcomer once, and then never again.
What you'll hear
If someone is wearing a different colour, they aren't lost. That's the libero.
DS

Defensive specialist

Also called: DS, back-row sub

Same idea as a libero — a back-row passer and digger — but they come on with an ordinary substitution, which means they can serve.

Where
Back row, usually swapped in for a middle blocker who has rotated back.
The job
Passes, digs, and serves. Often the steadiest passer on the team.
If you're new
A very common way to get court time while you're still learning to hit. Passing is the fastest skill to become useful at.
What you'll hear
“We'll put you back row” usually means this, and it isn't a demotion.

You don't have to pick one.

In pickup volleyball almost nobody plays a fixed position. Hosts put people where the team is short, and “I'll go wherever” is a completely normal answer — often the most useful one in the gym. Learn to pass first, and the rest sorts itself out.
